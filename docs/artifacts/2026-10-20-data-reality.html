The story so far

Stage 1 settled what we build: an agent that answers Meridian's questions from its own help files, books in its database, and hands the rest to people honestly. Stage 2 checks that against real inputs before any design.

We picked real calls and files that cover each kind of request, and especially the risky ones: an outdated file that looks like the right answer, a $49 fee that must come before a change, a gas-type emergency, a policy gap, and a task the systems can't finish. For each, we wrote down exactly what the agent must do. Where the call's customer isn't in the database, we kept their words and swapped in a real database customer, and we say so.

WP-2 · The $144 vs $149 after-hours fee You: explain as billed, $144

Where this comes up in the project

Omar asks what his $478 bill is for. After the code, the agent reads his invoice: diagnostic $89, contactor $245, after-hours $144.

The problem we're solving

Meridian's fee schedule says after-hours is $149, not $144. Should the agent point that out on its own, or only if Omar asks?

Why it's hard

Saying it invites a dispute and looks like the agent judging the bill. Staying silent hides a fact. The agent is told to explain, never judge.

Why we asked you, and what your answer changed

It sets how every invoice answer is worded and which billing tests count as correct.

Your answer ("wp2 144"): the agent explains the line as billed, $144, and doesn't raise $149 on its own. If Omar questions the line, the team can review it and the chat hands off. The mismatch always goes into the staff brief.

WP-3 · Which "AC not cooling" chats get the urgent path You: only when someone is in danger

Where this comes up in the project

Linda says her AC isn't cooling and wants a Saturday evening visit. The earlier rule sent every no-heat, no-cooling or leak report to the emergency number and a priority case.

The problem we're solving

About 53 of the 75 HVAC booking calls report no heat, no cooling or water pooling. Each would have opened a priority case and shown the emergency number, even when the customer only needs a visit.

Why it's hard

Priority cases are meant to stand out. If most bookings create one, staff stop noticing them. But a home that is dangerously hot or cold for someone vulnerable must not wait in the booking queue.

Why we asked you, and what your answer changed

It sets how many cases staff see, how the supervisor view counts priority work, and which booking tests expect the emergency number.

Your answer: the urgent path runs "only … if they're stating its an emergency as in someone is in danger as a result of the conditions described". Linda books normally. The short urgency questions stay, inside the booking, so a customer can say so. This narrows OD-4 (ii) and PRD R-8.

WP-6 · Add "turn the system off"? You: no

Where this comes up in the project

A customer smells burning from the vents but can't say whether a breaker tripped. The agent gives the cautious answer: the safety triggers, the emergency number, and a priority case.

The problem we're solving

Should the cautious answer also say "turn the system off at the thermostat"? It sounds sensible, but no Meridian help file says it for a burning smell.

Why it's hard

Safety advice we invent is advice the company never approved. The audit's rule is to add nothing beyond the help files. But a customer may expect a practical step.

Why we asked you, and what your answer changed

It sets the exact safety wording the agent uses and tests for. The parked code includes this line, so it is removed when the build starts.

Your answer ("i take only your recommendation"): no. Only the help files' words. The KB's "turn the system off" step stays limited to water pooling at the indoor unit.
