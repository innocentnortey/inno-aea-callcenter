The story so far

Meridian is a made-up heating, cooling and plumbing company in Austin. Its phone team can't keep up. A homeowner whose AC died in August waits on hold behind people asking for their balance. AEA wants an AI agent that takes the routine calls, so people can focus on the rest, without hiring more staff.

Today we read every one of the 420 sample calls, all 17 help documents and the booking database. We sorted the calls into 37 kinds and 11 families. We found which ones the supplied systems can answer, which ones must reach a person, and which ones need systems Meridian never gave us. We studied how service works for a home-services company, designed how a handoff to a person should work, and you made every product choice. This page puts it all in one place.

1 · The problem, step by step

A "why-chain" keeps asking "why?" until it reaches something we can change.

Callers wait Fact

The brief says staff "cannot keep up with call volume".

Briefing, Part B.

Every request needs a person Fact

Booking is 31% of calls, billing questions 16%, membership 14%, warranty 11%. All go to a person today.

Inventory §3.1.

There is no other way in Fact

All 420 are phone calls. Answers sit in messy documents. Bookings sit in a database only staff use.

Audit §3; transcript index.

What we can change Proposal

An agent that answers from current documents, books directly, and hands the rest to a person with its notes and an honest promise.

Not chosen: "agents are slow" (calls are short), "hire more" (the brief rules it out), "a better phone menu" (routes, fixes nothing).

2 · What we found in the data

These findings shaped every choice below. Each comes from a script over all 420 calls, not a sample.

Handoffs are callbacks Fact

54 calls (12.9%) need a person or a safety step. None was a live transfer. Every one asked for help in its first sentence.

Inventory §1, §3.3.

Promises were inconsistent Fact

The same kind of case got "today", "1 business day" or "2 business days". No reference numbers. No callback windows.

Inventory §1, §5.3.

Calls can't be checked Fact

None of the 131 account numbers, 130 phones or 42 invoices in the calls exists in the database. Only four test customers can be checked.

Inventory §1.

Some systems are missing Fact

74 calls needed things we don't have: payment links, autopay, auto-renew, card records, registration records, repair history.

Inventory §5.2.

Agents said things the policy doesn't Fact

A $60 quote gap explained by fees that aren't $60. 14 of 15 short-notice reschedules with no $49 fee. Tune-ups quoted the wrong price.

Inventory §5.3. The calls are not an answer key.

A safety gap Fact

16 callers smelled burning from the vents. All were booked as routine. The policy only covers burning with tripped breakers.

Inventory §3.2; KB safety policy.

Invoices can't show credits Fact

All 45 invoices with a diagnostic fee also bill a repair, with no credit line. No invoice links to its visit.

Inventory §10.2.

The data is templated Fact

420 calls come from 46 opening templates. Tests must hold out whole templates, or scores look better than they are.

Inventory §2; audit D-1.

4 · The traps we must not fall into

The brief's reviewer asks "Did you catch the traps?" These eight decide the grade. Each card says what we do about it.

Chasing a big number Your decision

Deflecting calls can look like success. We count only correct resolutions. Abandoned or wrong chats are failures.

Audit C-4, E-1; framing §2.

"Top 3" by volume Your decision

Big topics include disputes and payments we must not handle. We take the answerable part of each.

Audit C-2; PRD §3.2.

Old documents Your decision

Archive copies say $149 membership, 90-day warranty, refunds any time. They are left out of search entirely.

DEC-4; audit K-6, K-8.

Gas leak in a queue Your decision

The safety instruction comes first, in code, before any lookup, queue or case.

Audit C-7, E-3; D5.

Public link, real records Your decision

Private details need a one-time code sent to the contact on record (simulated). Rate limits and a spend cap.

Audit C-11, G-2; OD-3.

Calls as answer keys Fact

Agents' answers were often off-policy. Expected answers come from the current KB and database.

Audit D-5; inventory §5.3.

The real clock Fact

"Today" is Friday 2026-10-16 in this data. The agent uses that fixed date.

Dataset README; audit C-9.

Giving money away Your decision

The AI never grants a waiver or discount. Staff review contested fees.

5b · What the agent knows, and where it comes from

We don't write the knowledge base. Meridian gave us 17 help files. We read every file, split the current ones into small pieces, and turn each piece into numbers that capture its meaning (an "embedding"). Those go into a vector store: an index file inside the app that finds the pieces closest in meaning to a question. The agent answers only from what comes back, shows its source, and says so if nothing answers the question (PRD R-9 to R-12).

6 · What happens when a person takes over

Five parts make a handoff work, ranked. The first two are both needed: without the first nobody can reach a person, and without the second the person starts over.

C1 Honest human access Your decision

"Talk to a person" is always visible. The agent offers help early when things go wrong. It never pretends someone is online.

OD-1, OD-5, OD-6.

C2 A short brief Your decision

Eight fixed headings, about a fifth-grade reading level. Facts and the customer's claims are kept apart. The full chat is one click away.

Your direction P2, P7; consolidated C2.

C3 Someone owns every case Your decision

Every case has a state, an owner, a next step and a due time. Supervisors see what is unowned, due soon or overdue.

Your direction P5; consolidated §6.

C4 Sources you can check Your decision

Each fact links to what the AI saw and to the current record, with a "changed since" marker.

Your direction P6; D6.

C5 Outcomes and corrections Proposal

Staff record the outcome and flag AI mistakes into a review list. Nothing changes the AI automatically. First to cut if time runs short.

Consolidated C5; brief Part B §7.

Example brief (seeded test case) Proposal

  • Why this came to you: a disputed charge goes to a person.
  • What the customer needs: "Nobody told me about an after-hours fee. I want that taken off."
  • What we know for sure: invoice INV-204411 is $478, unpaid. Lines: diagnostic $89, contactor $245, after-hours $144. Our fee schedule says after-hours is $149.
  • What the customer says (not checked): the tech said $89 to come out.
  • What the AI did: explained each line; changed and promised nothing.
  • What we told the customer: "A representative will get back to you within 1 business day."
  • What you need to decide: should the after-hours charge stay? Should the $89 be credited?
  • Who and how: record MHC-10002, code entered (demo); phone, mornings.

Consolidated §5. Seeded test customer, not a real call.

Staffing, told honestly Your decision

  • Default: nobody online. No queue is offered. "A representative will get back to you within 1 business day."
  • Refund requests: "within 2 business days". That is a reply, not a refund.
  • Simulated shift: a labelled switch. A banner on both screens says no Meridian employee is online.
  • Two sessions: the reviewer chats as a customer in one tab and acts as "Riley (demo representative)" in another.
  • Follow-up: email by default, or phone if the customer prefers. Recorded, never sent.

OD-1, OD-2, OD-4, OD-5.

1 · The PRD's scope Recommend: yes

Where this comes up in the project

The brief asks the agent to answer "the top 3 call drivers" from the documents. We counted all 420 calls to find them (section 3).

The problem we're solving

Pick the three topics the agent answers. Example: "What does the membership include?" is topic A; "Why is my bill $478?" is topic B.

Why it's hard

The biggest topics are not all answerable. Billing includes payments we cannot take and disputes that policy keeps with people. So we take only the answerable part of each.

Why we're asking you, and what your answer changes

Every requirement and test case depends on these three topics. With your yes we build membership, billing-question and warranty answers, plus booking. A different pick changes the tests and the coverage number.

Recommended: membership, billing questions and warranty (PRD §3.2), with the must, should and could levels in PRD §4.

2 · Shortening the later stages for 06:00 Recommend: yes

Where this comes up in the project

The checklist has seven more signed stages before building: samples, hard problems, interview, design track, spikes, design and tickets.

The problem we're solving

Decide what replaces them tonight. Example: the 420-call inventory stands in for the data samples, and the build plan stands in for tickets.

Why it's hard

Skipping a stage loses its checks. But they cannot all run in about 10 hours. The checklist only allows a skip that you record yourself.

Why we're asking you, and what your answer changes

No code may start until this is recorded. With your yes, we write the shortcuts in the Skips table and start the build. With no, we follow every stage and miss 06:00.

Recommended: the shortcuts in PRD §10, item 2.

3 · The bars we measure against Recommend: yes

Where this comes up in the project

The scoring run will report numbers. The README and the video show them against these bars (section 8).

The problem we're solving

Set the bars. Example: if wrong warranty answers go over 5%, warranty questions go to people until fixed.

Why it's hard

We aim for 100% inside our scope, but a run will show the truth. A bar too strict cuts useful topics. One too loose lets wrong answers through.

Why we're asking you, and what your answer changes

The bars decide when a topic is ready or cut, and how we present results. Your yes sets them for the build and the demo.

Recommended: aim for 100% in scope, with ≥ 90% per topic before it counts as ready; wrong answers ≤ 5%; the right document found ≥ 90% of the time.

4 · Five system questions Recommend the answers below

Where this comes up in the project

Before we size anything on Railway, the house rules say you answer five questions about load, peaks, failures, money and risk.

The problem we're solving

Set what the deployed demo must handle. Example: how many reviewers chat at once, and what happens if the server restarts.

Why it's hard

The brief gives a test, not a load. Guessing a number would look like a choice you made. That is the mistake these rules exist to stop.

Why we're asking you, and what your answer changes

They set the database setup, the spend cap and the failure tests. You have answered load and peak. The other three still need a yes or a change before the build starts.

  • Load: your answer, 2026-10-20. About 100 conversations a day with the agent, as a big regional company.
  • Peak: your answer, 2026-10-20. A spike of about 20 users at once.
  • Failure to survive: a server restart, with no lost chats or cases.
  • Spending: Railway on your plan; model spend capped daily (suggest $10 a day).
  • Hardest thing to prove: that every number we report is honest.
