The story so far

Meridian Home Comfort is a made-up heating, cooling and plumbing company in Austin. Its customers wait on the phone to do simple things. They book a repair, ask what a bill is for, or ask what the membership covers. AEA asked us to handle most of those chats with an AI agent, without hurting anyone.

AEA also asks two hard questions. Is the containment number real? (Containment is the share of chats the AI finishes with no person.) And would the business know if the system broke? This design answers the second question as carefully as it builds the agent.

Stages 1 to 4 fixed what we build. A public chat agent answers membership, billing and warranty questions from the current knowledge base. It books visits in a copy of the scheduling database, never the original. It says the safety instruction first. Everything else goes to a person, with an honest promise taken from a policy table. A staff console lets a reviewer play the person who picks up. You set the size: about 100 chats a day, a spike of about 20 at once, and a written plan for 10 times that, with nothing built for it.

At Stage 3 you drew one turn yourself. A turn is one customer message and the agent's reply. Code checks go first. Then a planner, a model that only chooses, decides "just talk" or "act". Specialists act with narrow tools. Code decides whether to hand off. Last, a responder, a model with no tools, writes the words.

Stage 5 built a thin copy of that turn and ran it. We call it spike SP-1. A spike is a quick throwaway build that answers one question. It was correct: 0 hard-gate failures in 190 scored chats [M]. It was cheap: $0.016 a chat [M]. Speed was the risk. One turn chains three or four model calls. With 20 chats at once, the p95 turn took 10.2 s [M]. You chose mix B: Jev, a small fast model reached through OpenRouter, plans and checks for danger, and Claude Sonnet 5.5 writes. You set the line at p95 ≤ 12.5 s at 20 at once [T].

SP-1 also found one real bug. In worked pair WP-4F, a customer moves a visit that is more than a day away, so no fee applies. Turn 3 moved the visit. In turn 4 she wrote "Yes, please." The responder saw no booking change in that turn, so it told her the move had not happened. This design's main job is to make that impossible. It also keeps the Sonnet calls down and puts a deadline on every turn.

What the trace teaches us

1 · Slow steps don't add up, but they can land together Fact

The step p95s added together are 0.4 + 4.1 + 8.4 = 12.9 s [C], over the line. The measured turn p95 is 10.2 s [M], because a slow specialist and a slow responder rarely hit the same turn. When they do, the turn passes 12.5 s. That is why the hard deadline sits well above the line, not on it.

2 · SP-1's p95 mixes every kind of turn Proposal

A handoff turn like T4 has the longest reply and a brief call beside it, so it probably sits in the slower part of the spread [A]. Stage 9 must report p95 per turn type: talk, act, handoff, safety and code entry, not one pooled number.

3 · A turn mostly waits, so capacity means threads, not CPU Fact

A turn holds one pool thread for its whole life, and two short SQLite locks. The CPU is idle almost the whole time. DO-3 adds that the two side-by-side steps borrow up to two LangGraph helper threads, which are outside the 40-thread pool [A].

4 · Load and bottlenecks, today and at 10×

One tool helps here. Little's Law says the number of jobs in progress equals arrivals per second times seconds per job. If one shopper reaches the till every 2 seconds and each takes 4 seconds, about 2 are at the till at any moment.

7.3 Conversation state: how R-45 carries finished actions

Before this design, the responder learned what happened only from this turn's facts. Now it gets two separate inputs. Turn facts say what this turn did, and reset every turn. settled says everything the chat has done, read from the actions and cases tables at the start of every turn. Each row holds the action kind (booked, moved, cancelled, change request sent, case opened, follow-up saved, verified), the record IDs, the before and after values, the turn ID, the time and an idempotency key (a tag that stops the same write happening twice).

The words and the panel can't disagree Proposal

The R-46 completion check compares claims against settled. The CON-2 settled object that drives the chat's "What's settled" panel is built from the same rows (N-4, N-27). The DB rows are the authority; the checkpoint holds a copy for the graph.

8.3 The 60 s deadline, in detail

What 60 s means in practice Your decision

  • The target is unchanged: p95 ≤ 12.5 s at 20 at once [T]; measured 10.2 s [M]. The deadline is only the backstop.
  • It leaves room for slow tool work and two or more retries inside the turn.
  • The chat says "Still working on this…" at about 10 s. Until 60 s the customer keeps a working message box and the "Talk to a person" key.
  • A turn can now hold a worker thread for up to 60 s. The 30-turn admission limit stays under the 40-thread pool, so slow turns cannot use them all.
  • SP-1 used a 45 s deadline and had 0 unrecovered turns [M].
  • Railway allows it: its HTTP request limit is 15 minutes (S-232), so a 60 s turn and the browser's 65 s give-up fit.

SDQ-1, your decision 2026-10-21; DO-1 §8.1.

How the deadline works Proposal

  • Each model call gets the smaller of its role cap and the time left. Proposed caps: Jev 3 s (its p95 is 0.4 s [M]), specialist step 8 s, responder 12 s, brief 12 s.
  • The SDK's own retries are off, because it waits as long as any "retry after" says. Our loop retries once, only for retryable errors (408, 409, 429 with a retry time, 5xx, 529), and only when the wait fits in the time left.
  • The graph checks the deadline between steps. A database write that has started always finishes; the run stops at the next step boundary.
  • The timeout reply is code, not a model. It then reports whatever settled holds, so a booking that went through is still reported, and offers "Try again" (same turn ID) and "Talk to a person".
  • "Try again" is repeat-safe: an idempotency key on each action stops a second write.
  • Two timeouts in one chat count as a failure signal (OD-6): the next reply offers a person.
  • The browser gives up after deadline + 5 s [A] and shows "That didn't send. Your message is still here."

DO-1 §8.1; DO-3 §2.3 (browser waits 65 s).
